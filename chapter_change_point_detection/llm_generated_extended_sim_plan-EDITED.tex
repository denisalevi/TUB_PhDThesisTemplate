\section{Context within thesis}

The previous chapter introduced the parallel pathway theory (PPT) as a circuit mechanism for systems memory consolidation. In that model, parallel pathways learn with different effective learning rates, leading to different degrees of semantic generalization. The present chapter asks what such a diversity of learning rates could be useful for when the statistics of the environment change over time. The chapter uses Bayesian change-point detection as a normative framework for learning in changing environments, and suggests that a diversity in learning rates during systems consolidation could be part of a neural implementation thereof.

\section{Introduction}

Memories allow us to predict the future based on the past. This requires the integration of previous experiences into an internal model of the world. Such integration is thought to occur during systems memory consolidation, where new experiences stored in the hippocampus are gradually integrated into neocortical knowledge structures \citep{McClelland1995}. This is especially relevant when learning the statistical regularities of the environment. However, the world and its statistics are not stationary. When the environment changes, old experiences can become misleading, and predictions that were useful before may no longer be appropriate. For example, if the commute to work used to be reliably quick but then roadworks start, the previous distribution of travel times is no longer the right basis for predicting how long the commute will take tomorrow.

A principled solution to this problem is provided by Bayesian change-point detection algorithms \citep{Adams2007}. These algorithms infer whether a change in the statistics of the environment has occurred and adjust predictions by weighting evidence from different possible intervals since the last change. This allows an agent to combine two requirements that are in tension: integrating evidence over long periods when the world is stable, but rapidly discounting old evidence when the world changes.

Exact Bayesian change-point detection is computationally expensive because it requires maintaining predictive distributions for different possible run lengths. Several approximations have therefore been proposed. \citet{Nassar2010} reduced Bayesian updating to a delta-rule update with an adaptive learning rate. A mixture of fixed-learning-rate estimators can also approximate the full Bayesian solution \citep{Wilson2013}. This latter approximation is especially relevant here, because systems memory consolidation theory suggests different learning rates in the consolidation hierarchy \citep{McClelland1995,Roxin2013}, and PPT introduces a learning-rate dependent semantic generalization strength (Ch.~\ref{ch:ppt}). In this chapter, we ask whether learning-rate diversity in systems consolidation could provide a biological basis for the multiple evidence-window estimates required by approximate change-point inference.

\section{Learning a Bernoulli contingency in a changing environment}

We consider the simplest non-trivial case: a binary observation $x_t \in \{0,1\}$ at trial $t$, drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with success probability $\theta_t$,
\begin{equation}
    p(x_t \mid \theta_t) = \theta_t^{x_t}(1-\theta_t)^{1-x_t}.
\end{equation}
The latent parameter $\theta_t$ is piecewise constant: it takes a fixed value during each stable regime and resets at change points (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bernoulli-task}A). The learner only observes the binary sequence $x_{1:t} = (x_1, \dots, x_t)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bernoulli-task}B) and has to predict the next observation $x_{t+1}$ without knowing the change points.

If the environment were stationary, $\theta_t$ would be constant and a Beta prior with pseudo-counts $a_0$ and $b_0$ would give a conjugate Beta posterior,
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta) = \frac{1}{B(a_0,b_0)}\theta^{a_0-1}(1-\theta)^{b_0-1},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta \mid x_{1:t})
    = \frac{1}{B(a_t,b_t)}\theta^{a_t-1}(1-\theta)^{b_t-1},
\end{equation}
where $B(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the beta function and the running counts
\begin{equation}
    a_t = a_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{t}x_i,
    \qquad
    b_t = b_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-x_i)
\end{equation}
accumulate successes and failures over the full history. The posterior predictive mean is then $\hat\theta_t = a_t/(a_t+b_t)$. This stationary learner is useful when the environment is genuinely stable, but it averages over all past observations and therefore adapts too slowly when the contingency changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bernoulli-task}C, red line).

\section{Bayesian online change-point detection}
\label{sec:cpd-bocpd}

Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD) addresses non-stationarity by augmenting the state with the run length $r_t$, defined as the number of observations since the most recent change point \citep{Adams2007}. The learner maintains a separate posterior over $\theta$ conditional on each possible run length, which in the Bernoulli case is again a Beta density,
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta \mid r_t=r, x_{1:t})
    = \frac{1}{B(a_t^{(r)},b_t^{(r)})}\,\theta^{a_t^{(r)}-1}(1-\theta)^{b_t^{(r)}-1}.
\end{equation}
Here $a_t^{(r)}$ and $b_t^{(r)}$ are the success and failure counts the learner would have accumulated if the current run had length $r$, plus the prior pseudo-counts $a_0$ and $b_0$. They form the sufficient statistics under hypothesis $r$ and are much more compact than the full history.

The predictive probability of a success under a single run-length hypothesis is
\begin{equation}
    p(x_{t+1}=1 \mid r_t=r, x_{1:t})
    = \int_0^1 \theta\,p(\theta \mid r_t=r,x_{1:t})\,d\theta
    = \frac{a_t^{(r)}}{a_t^{(r)}+b_t^{(r)}},
\end{equation}
and the full Bayesian prediction averages these estimates over the run-length posterior,
\begin{equation}
    p(x_{t+1}=1 \mid x_{1:t})
    = \sum_r \frac{a_t^{(r)}}{a_t^{(r)}+b_t^{(r)}}\,p(r_t=r \mid x_{1:t}).
\end{equation}

The run-length posterior itself follows a simple recursive update. Between two observations the current run either continues, in which case $r_t = r_{t-1}+1$ and the Beta counts are updated with $x_t$, or a change point occurs, in which case $r_t = 0$ and the sufficient statistics reset to the prior pseudo-counts. The prior probability of a change is set by the hazard function
\begin{equation}
    H(r) = p(r_t=0 \mid r_{t-1}=r-1),
\end{equation}
which gives the probability that a run ends after surviving to age $r$. Throughout this chapter we use a constant hazard $H(r)=h$, corresponding to a geometric prior over regime durations; the probability of a change on the next trial is then independent of how long the current regime has already lasted.

Combining the predictive likelihood, the hazard and the sufficient statistics gives the joint update over $(r_t, a_t^{(r)}, b_t^{(r)})$ that is computed online. Figure~\ref{fig:cpd-bocpd-internals} shows the resulting internal variables for the example sequence of Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bernoulli-task}. The run-length posterior places its mass on a diagonal ridge that grows linearly during each stable regime, resets to small $r_t$ shortly after each change point, and occasionally bifurcates because the learner is initially uncertain which of the surviving run-length hypotheses is correct (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bocpd-internals}A). The run-length-conditional means $a_t^{(r)}/(a_t^{(r)}+b_t^{(r)})$ track the latent $\theta_t$ at very different speeds: short run lengths follow recent observations closely and fluctuate, while long run lengths integrate over many trials and respond slowly (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bocpd-internals}B). The marginal predictive after summing over run lengths recovers fast adaptation after each change point (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bocpd-internals}C). Under constant hazard, the posterior mass on $r_t=0$ is uninformative because it equals the hazard at every trial; we therefore use the recent-run probability $p(r_t \leq 5 \mid x_{1:t})$ as a data-driven change indicator, which peaks shortly after each true change point (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-bocpd-internals}D).

Although we use Bernoulli observations for clarity, the same logic generalizes to any conjugate exponential-family observation model: the counts $a_t^{(r)}$ and $b_t^{(r)}$ are simply the sufficient statistics of the Bernoulli likelihood and are replaced by the corresponding sufficient statistics for Gaussian, Poisson or other observation models.

\section{A finite pathway approximation}
\label{sec:cpd-finite-pathway}

The exact BOCPD posterior is supported on every possible run length up to the current trial. A biological system is unlikely to maintain such a trellis explicitly. Approximations that replace the full trellis with a small number of fixed-timescale estimates have therefore been proposed \citep{Nassar2010, Wilson2013}, and it is this class of approximations that connects most directly to the parallel-pathway architecture of PPT.

We approximate the posterior by a bank of $K$ pathway-like estimates
\begin{equation}
    \hat\theta_t^{(k)}, \qquad k=1,\ldots,K,
\end{equation}
each updated by a delta rule with a fixed learning rate $\alpha_k$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat\theta_t^{(k)} = \hat\theta_{t-1}^{(k)} + \alpha_k\,(x_t - \hat\theta_{t-1}^{(k)}).
\end{equation}
Each $\alpha_k$ defines an effective timescale $\tau_k = 1/\alpha_k$ over which observations contribute to that pathway's estimate. A downstream readout combines them into a single prediction,
\begin{equation}
    \hat\theta_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_t^{(k)}\,\hat\theta_t^{(k)},
    \qquad
    \sum_k w_t^{(k)} = 1.
\end{equation}
We update the readout weights $w_t^{(k)}$ by Bayesian filtering, using the predictive likelihood $p(x_t \mid \hat\theta_{t-1}^{(k)})$ of each pathway and a small leak toward the uniform distribution to allow the readout to re-explore the bank after change points.

We compare three predictors against exact BOCPD on the example sequence (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-finite-bank}): a single delta-rule learner with intermediate rate $\alpha = 0.1$; a three-pathway bank with rates $\alpha \in \{1/4, 1/14, 1/82\}$, chosen so that fast, intermediate and slow timescales span the run lengths seen in the data; and the reduced mixture-of-delta-rules of \citet{Wilson2013}, which uses 18 log-spaced run-length nodes between $r=1$ and $r=100$ and propagates the run-length distribution through a node-to-node transition matrix that respects both growth and reset transitions.

The pathway estimates display the timescale separation that motivates them (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-finite-bank}B): the fast pathway tracks every fluctuation in $\theta_t$ but is noisy within a regime; the slow pathway is smooth but lags strongly after change points; the intermediate pathway lies between the two. The Bayesian readout exploits this complementarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-finite-bank}C). Immediately after a change point, when the slow pathway has been driven far from the true contingency, the fast pathway has higher predictive likelihood and the readout weights shift toward it. Once the environment has been stable for a while the slow pathway becomes more accurate, the readout shifts back toward it, and the intermediate pathway tends to dominate at intermediate times. The resulting mixture prediction closely follows exact BOCPD and matches it in mean predictive negative log-likelihood (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-finite-bank}D, E). The reduced Wilson model achieves the best score in this example because its transition matrix encodes the same geometric reset structure as BOCPD but with a flatter effective hazard than the value we passed to BOCPD; the single-rate baseline is clearly worse than the multi-timescale predictors. A small bank of fixed-timescale learners therefore captures the qualitative behaviour of the full run-length posterior.

\section{From run lengths to pathway estimates}

The run-length posterior of exact BOCPD already has a natural interpretation in terms of evidence windows. A short run length means that only recent observations should be trusted, a long run length that a longer history can be integrated, and the Bayesian prediction is a mixture over all such windows weighted by their posterior probability. Replacing the full trellis with a finite bank of fixed-timescale delta rules makes this interpretation concrete: each pathway corresponds to one effective window and the readout weights play the role of the run-length posterior on a reduced grid.

The parallel-pathway architecture of PPT provides exactly such a bank. Fast pathways are dominated by recent observations, slow pathways integrate over longer histories, and the consolidation gradient from hippocampus to cortex naturally arranges them along a continuum of effective learning rates (Ch.~\ref{ch:ppt}; Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-pathway-interpretation}E). A pathway does not literally represent one value of $r_t$, but the set of pathway estimates can serve as a basis from which a downstream readout approximates the run-length mixture used by exact change-point inference (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpd-pathway-interpretation}A--D). This reframes the learning-rate diversity of systems consolidation from a mechanism solely for graded semantic generalization to one that simultaneously supports adaptive prediction in changing environments.

\section{Discussion}

Adaptive learning in a changing environment requires that the learner decides how much of the past remains relevant before it updates. A surprising observation can reflect either noise within the current regime or a true change in the latent state, and the two interpretations call for very different revisions of the predictive distribution. BOCPD captures this requirement in its purest form: it maintains a posterior over the run length and predicts by mixing run-length-conditional estimates. Reading the Bernoulli model in this way makes the computational requirement explicit. Each evidence window contributes an estimate, and an adaptive prediction is a weighted combination of these estimates.

The finite-pathway approximation gives this requirement a biologically interpretable form. A small bank of fixed-rate delta-rule learners reproduces the prediction performance of exact BOCPD on the Bernoulli task and recovers the same qualitative dynamics as the reduced mixture of \citet{Wilson2013}. This matters for the consolidation story because PPT already postulates such a bank: parallel pathways with graded effective learning rates from hippocampus to cortex (Ch.~\ref{ch:ppt}). The chapter therefore proposes a new normative role for the learning-rate diversity introduced by PPT. The same architecture that supports graded semantic generalization can also serve as the multi-timescale basis required for approximate change-point inference.

Pathways are unlikely to carry the full change-point computation on their own. Exact BOCPD also requires a posterior over run lengths and, if the hazard is uncertain, additional beliefs over the hazard parameters. A biologically realistic decomposition therefore distributes the ideal-observer ingredients across systems: hippocampal--cortical pathways provide candidate evidence-window estimates, while frontal, cingulate, striatal and neuromodulatory systems contribute the signals required to weight or gate them. Several neural results are consistent with such a distributed implementation. Activity in anterior cingulate cortex tracks environmental volatility and the value of information in changing environments \citep{Behrens2007}; functionally dissociable signals related to changepoint probability, relative uncertainty and reward influence learning rate \citep{McGuire2014}; and dorsomedial frontal cortex carries value estimates simultaneously at multiple timescales \citep{Meder2017}. These observations support the idea that the brain represents ingredients of adaptive learning across multiple regions rather than computing the run-length posterior in any single area.

The hazard function is an assumption about the environment, not a property of the observer, and changing it changes the inference problem rather than the pathway basis. A constant hazard treats every trial as equally likely to terminate a regime; a decreasing hazard expresses the belief that long-lived regimes are more likely to persist; an increasing hazard expresses the belief that regimes have typical lifetimes. Any age-dependent hazard $H(r)$ can be plugged into the same BOCPD recursion, because $r_t$ is the relevant state. When the hazard is fixed but unknown the observer can infer it jointly with the run length, and when it varies over time a hierarchical model is needed \citep{Wilson2010}.

The relation between heavy-tailed memory and heavy-tailed hazards deserves a separate clarifying remark. A power-law-like hazard is a statement about environmental durations, whereas a power-law-like memory kernel produced by a broad distribution of pathway learning rates is a property of the observer. The two can coincide in particular regimes but they are different objects. For the consolidation story the useful claim is therefore weaker and more robust: a broad, log-spaced set of pathway timescales provides a flexible approximation basis when the relevant environmental timescale is uncertain, regardless of the precise hazard.

Finally, the formal derivation assumes that all pathway estimates receive the same online observation stream, which is a known simplification for systems consolidation. Replay can reshape the effective evidence kernel of each pathway by selectively resampling past observations, for example emphasising post-change observations after an inferred change point or preserving older evidence during stable periods. Integrating replay with the pathway-mixture view of change-point inference is a natural extension that is left to future work.

\section{Chapter summary}

Adaptive prediction in changing environments requires the learner to combine estimates over multiple possible evidence windows, as made explicit by the run-length posterior of BOCPD. In the Bernoulli case, these estimates are Beta posteriors with run-length-specific counts $a_t^{(r)}$ and $b_t^{(r)}$. A small bank of fixed-rate delta-rule learners reproduces the predictive performance of exact BOCPD and the qualitative dynamics of the reduced mixture of \citet{Wilson2013}, with readout weights that shift toward fast pathways after change points and toward slow pathways during stable periods. This identifies the learning-rate diversity proposed by PPT as a biologically plausible approximation basis for change-point inference, and gives that diversity an additional normative role beyond graded semantic generalization.
