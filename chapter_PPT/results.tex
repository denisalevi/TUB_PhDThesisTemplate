We conceptualize a memory as a cue-response association that is stored in a synaptic path- way between an input area – hosting a neural representation of the cue – and an output area – hosting a neural representation of the response. We hypothesize that such a memory can be consolidated by a transfer of the association to a parallel synaptic pathway through Hebbian plasticity, by imprinting a suitable set of synaptic configurationweights. We first illustrate the basic mechanism in a simple hippocampal circuit motif and then show that it can be iterated in a cascade to achieve a full consolidation into neocortex.

The proposed theory relies on a parallel structure of feedforward connections onto the same output area: a direct pathway and an indirect, multisynaptic pathway with slightly longer temporal delay. Memories are initially stored in the indirect pathway and subsequently transferred to the direct pathway via Hebbian plasticity in synapses targeting the output area, constituting the first consolidation step. The mechanism can be exemplified in the hippocampal formation as follows: We assume that a memory is initially stored in the indirect (i.e., multisynaptic) path- way from EC through DG and CA3, and reaching CA1 through the Schaffer collaterals [SC; Fig. 1A, blue; 14; 15]. The memory can be recalled by a specific neural activity pattern in EC – a cue – that triggers spikes in a subset of CA1 cells through the indirect pathway – the associ- ated response. The same cue also reaches CA1 through the parallel direct (i.e., monosynaptic) PPCA1 (Fig. 1A, red). Initially, this direct input fails to trigger spikes, because the synaptic weight pattern in the PPCA1 does not match the cue. However, PPCA1 inputs that were acti- vated by the cue precede the CA1 spikes triggered by the indirect pathway by 5-15 ms [16] (Fig. 1B), a delay which favours selective long-term potentiation due to spike timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)[17–19]. Consequently, cue-driven PPCA1 synapses are strengthened until the memory that was initially stored in the indirect pathway can be recalled via the direct pathway alone.

\subsection{Consolidation of spatial representations}
\label{sec:results_linear_track}

%The mathematical analysis of the PPT makes two key predictions. First, it suggests that STDP in a parallel direct pathway achieves consolidation by performing a linear regression between inputs in the direct and the indirect pathways [Eqs~\eqref{eq:fixed point dynamics} and~\eqref{eq:error function dynamics}]. Therefore, the proposed mechanism should generalize to situations in which the cue representations in the direct and indirect pathways differ. Second, the theory suggests that consolidation is most effective when the correlation time constants of the input during consolidation is matched to the coincidence time scale of STDP (Fig~\ref{sfig:theory_timing}B). In the following, we will show in simulations that those predictions hold and, moreover, that the mechanism is robust to neuronal nonlinearities.

So far, we illustrated the PPT in a simple integrate-and-fire model, in which the neural representations in the two pathways were identical, leading to a very strong correlation between the inputs to CA1. We next show the robustness of the suggested mechanism in a more biologically plausible setting.


To begin with, we show that the mechanism is robust to differing cue representations in the two pathways and to weaker correlations among them \cite{ONeill2017}. To this end, we used place cell representations \cite{OKeefe1971} for the SC input from CA3 and grid cell representations \cite{Hafting2005, Moser2008} for the \ppca input from EC (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}A).
Moreover, we show that the suggested mechanism is compatible with the biophysical properties of CA1 neurons, which receive inputs in different subcellular compartments. To this end, we simulated a multicompartmental CA1 pyramidal cell (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}B) that was endowed with active ion channels supporting backpropagating action potentials and dendritic calcium spikes (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}C, Methods Sec.~\ref{ssec:ppt_methods_multicompartment}).

The use of spatial representations in the input pathways allows us to consider simple forms of memories in a navigational context in which a given location on a linear track is associated with the activity of a given CA1 cell. Effectively, such an association generates a CA1 place cell. In line with the PPT, we assumed that the spatial selectivity of this CA1 place cell is initially determined solely by the indirect pathway via the SC, i.e., by place cell input from CA3. The goal of systems memory consolidation is then to transfer this spatial association to the direct input, which reaches the CA1 cell via the \ppca\ derived from grid cells in EC. In other words, place-cell input should supervise grid-cell input to develop a place-cell tuning.
Note that we use the spatial setup primarily as an illustration of the theory. We do not make claims regarding the temporal development of CA1 place cells \emph{in vivo}, which is not fully understood \cite{Solstad2006, OKeefe2021, Tukker2022}.

SC place field inputs were modelled by synapses that were active only in a small region of the track, whereas individual \ppca~grid cell inputs were active in multiple, evenly spaced regions along the track (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}A). The cue representation in the two pathways is now different, but correlated, because the same location is encoded. \todo[inline]{Double check that the correlation argument makes sense without theory, and potentially reference theory section here.}
The SC and \ppca~inputs projected to proximal and distal dendrites, respectively (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}B, \cite{Stuart2007}).
Synapses were initialized such that the SC input conductances were spatially tuned and resulted in place field-like activity in the CA1 cell while the \ppca~input had no spatial tuning (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}D).

During consolidation, SC and \ppca~input to the CA1 cell consisted of replays of previously encountered sequences of locations \cite{Skaggs1996, Lee2002}, with a replay speed 20 times faster than physical motion \cite{Lee2002}.
During replay, the SC input led to somatic spikes, which in turn triggered backpropagating action potentials that caused calcium spikes in the distal dendrites where the \ppca~synapses arrive (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}C and~E, \cite{Larkum1999}).
Through synaptic plasticity, \ppca~synapses active in the place field of the neuron were potentiated.
Over time, the \ppca~input adopted the spatial tuning of the SC input (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}F, left) and reproduced the original SC-induced place field output (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}F, right) with high correlation (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}G). This result is in line with our theoretical prediction that the direct pathway should attain the best possible linear approximation of the indirect pathway (Sec.~\ref{ssec:theory_stdp}). The spatial tuning of the the inputs is not perfectly matched because the approximation is bounded by the finite range of frequencies of the entorhinal inputs (in analogy to reconstructing a high-frequency signal, e.g. a narrow peak, with a finite set of Fourier components), which causes the ringing next to the target peak in Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}F (left). In summary, the PPT mechanism therefore consolidated associations even though the spatial representations in the two pathways differed and although the two pathways targeted different neuronal compartments with different numbers of synapses in the CA1 neuron with complex morphology.

The theory also predicts that consolidation is most effective when the correlation time in the input is matched to the time scale of STDP (Sec.~\ref{ssec:theory_timing} and Fig~\ref{sfig:theory_timing}B). In line with this prediction, consolidation failed when replay speed was reduced to that of physical motion (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial}G) because the time scale of rate changes in place and grid cell activity is then much longer than the delay between the two pathways and the time scale of STDP (Fig~\ref{sfig:theory_timing}C). Accelerated replay during sleep \cite{Lee2002} hence supports systems memory consolidation within the PPT by aligning the time scales of neural activity and synaptic plasticity \cite{DAlbis2015}.

\subsection{Consolidation of place-object associations in multiple hippocampal stages}
\label{sec:ppt-results-remondes}

Ultimately, to consolidate memories into neocortex, they have to move beyond the \ppca.
Notably, the \ppca~is itself part of an indirect pathway from EC to the subiculum (SUB) that is shortcut by a direct connection from EC to SUB (referenced as \ppsub; Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}A, left; \cite{Amaral1993}).
This suggests that the PPT can be reiterated to further consolidate memories from the \ppca~to the \ppsub~and beyond.

To illustrate this idea, we considered a standard paradigm for memory research in rodents: the Morris water maze
\cite{Morris1982}.
In the water maze, the rodent needs to find a submerged platform (object), i.e., it must store an object-place association.
Thus this paradigm requires neural representations of objects (such as the submerged platform) and places.
We hence constructed a model in which subregions of the hippocampal formation included neurons that encode places and neurons that encoded the identity of objects (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}A, right).

For simplicity and computational efficiency we switched to a rate-based neuron model (Methods Sec.~\ref{ssec:ppt_methods_lesion}). An object was chosen from a set of 128 different objects and placed in a circular open field environment (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}B, top).
As motivated by experiments \cite{Brun2002, Nakazawa2003, Nakashiba2008}, we implemented object-to-place associations in our model by enhancing, as before, synaptic connections in the SC, but now between object-encoding neurons in CA3 and place-specific neurons in CA1 (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}A, right). Here, we did not consider place-to-object associations. These are less relevant for the water maze task, where the task is to recall the location of a given object --- the platform --- rather than to recall which object was encountered at a given location. We tested object-to-place associations stored in the SC by activating the object representation in EC --- as a memory cue --- and determining the activities in CA1, triggered by the SC alone.
From these activities we inferred a spatial probability map of the recalled object location (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}B).

We first stored a single object-place association in the SC. During a subsequent consolidation  cycle --- representing one night --- place and object representations in EC were then randomly and independently activated.
Consistent with our previous results, the object-place association was gradually consolidated from the SC to the \ppca: after one night of consolidation, the correct spatial probability map of an object location was inferrable from CA1 activity triggered by the \ppca~alone (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}B).

To track the consolidation process over longer times, we assumed that a new random object-place association is stored in the SC every day.
This caused a decay of previous SC memory traces due to interference with newly stored associations (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}C, \cite{Lux2016, Fusi2007}).
During the night following each day, associations in the SC were partially consolidated into the \ppca, such that the consolidated association could be decoded from the \ppca~after a single night, but previously consolidated associations were not entirely overwritten.
As a result, object-place associations were maintained in the \ppca~for longer periods than in the SC, thus extending their memory lifetime (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}C).
Eventually, a  given \ppca~memory trace would also degrade as new interfering memories from the SC are consolidated.
However, as noted above, the \ppca~itself is part of an indirect pathway from EC to the SUB, for which there is in turn a parallel, direct perforant pathway \ppsub.
The association in the \ppca~(and SC) could therefore, in turn, be partially consolidated into the \ppsub, further extending memory lifetime (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}C).
Note that the extension of memory lifetime is supported in the model by a reduced plasticity in \ppsub\ compared to \ppca.

The model suggests that the \ppca~serves as a transient memory buffer that mediates a further consolidation into additional shortcut pathways downstream.
This hypothesis is supported by navigation studies in rats.
Using \ppca~lesions, Remondes and Schuman \cite{Remondes2004} have shown that the \ppca~is not required for the original acquisition of spatial memories, but that it is critically involved in their long-term maintenance.
However, lesioning the \ppca~21 days after acquiring a memory did not disrupt spatial memories, suggesting that the \ppca~is not the final storage site (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}D) and further supporting the idea that the \ppca\ is important to enable a transition from short-term to long-term memories.

To test whether our model could reproduce these experimental results, we simulated \ppca~lesions either before the acquisition of an object-place association or 21 days later.
Assuming that the rat's spatial exploration is determined by the probability map of the object location \cite{Herrnstein1961}, the model provided predictions for the time spent in different quadrants of the environment, which were in quantitative agreement with the data for all experimental conditions (Fig~\ref{fig:lesion}D).
Our model thus suggests that a hierarchical reiteration of parallel shortcuts --- the central circuit motif of the PPT --- could explain these experiments.

Similar to lesioning the \ppca, we predict that lesioning  \ppsub\ also has an impact on memory consolidation: \ppsub\ should act as a transient memory buffer but on a longer timescale than \ppca. In general, lesioning a pathway with a set of synapses that cover a specific range of time scales such that there is a ``gap'' should result in an impairment of consolidation if the lesion is done before the memory has ``moved on''. To illustrate this idea in more detail, we study in the next section a model with many stages in a hierarchy.


\subsection{Consolidation from hippocampus into neocortex by a hierarchical nesting of consolidation circuits}
\label{sec:ppt-results-hierarchy}

Given that shortcut connections are widespread throughout the brain \cite{VanEssen1992, Morgenstern2016, Constantinople2013}, we next hypothesized that a reiteration of the PPT can also achieve systems consolidation from hippocampus into neocortex.
To test this hypothesis, we studied a network model (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}A, Methods Sec.~\ref{ssec:ppt_methods_hierarchy}), in which the hippocampus (now simplified to a single area) receives input from a hierarchy of cortical areas, representing, e.g., a sensory system. It provides output to a different hierarchy of areas, representing, e.g., the motor system or another sensory system.

The network also contained shortcut connections that bypassed the hippocampus.
As in the previous section, new memories were stored in the hippocampus but not in any other indirect connection in the hierarchy.
The repeated storage of new memories every day leads to a decay of previously stored hippocampal memories. But memories are also consolidated by Hebbian plasticity in parallel pathways.

Tracing a specific memory over time revealed a gradual consolidation into the cortical shortcut connections, forming a ``memory wave'' \cite{Roxin2013} that travels from hippocampus into neocortex (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}B, Theory Sec.~\ref{ssec:theory_hierarchy}).
By exponentially decreasing the shortcut learning rate with distance from the hippocampus, a power-law decay of memories can be observed in the union of all shortcuts, e.g., by reading out the shortcut with the strongest memory trace at any moment in time (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}B). This observation is in line with a rich history of psychological studies on the mathematical shape of forgetting curves \cite{Wixted2004}. Note that for the readout we tried to make as few assumptions as possible by letting all pathways contribute on an equal footing. Taking the maximum over the pathways (as well as the mean) generates a power law.
Notably, we achieved memory retention times of years through only a small number ($\sim$5) of iterations of the PPT.
Finally, we found that memory retrieval accelerates during consolidation (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}C), in line with consolidation studies for motor skills \cite{Walker2002}. In our consolidation model, the time to recall decreases because the path from peripheral input to output becomes shorter through the use of more direct (peripheral) shortcut connections (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}A and B).

The predicted consolidation-mediated decrease of the time to recall critically depends on the utilized plasticity rule (STDP), which uses timing of input and output of neurons, and on our assumption that memories are initially acquired in an indirect pathway with a longer delay than direct pathways. While this assumption is reasonable for declarative memories that are initially stored in the hippocampus and then consolidated in sensory or motor areas towards neocortex, the underlying computational reasons for such a strategy are unknown. The strategy of the initial storage of memories in a pathway with a longer transmission delay could be related to the Complementary Learning Systems Theory (CLST) \cite{McClelland1995, McClelland2013} if the initial storage needs some preprocessing, e.g., to achieve representations that are suited for one-trial learning, e.g. population-sparse representations \cite{Leibold2008}. In general, our results do not imply that the reduction of delay is a central goal of systems memory consolidation or that it is even necessary. Reduction of delay may, however, be a nice side effect of systems memory consolidation with timing-based plasticity rules \cite{Song2000}. And such a reduction of delay does not need to be restricted to declarative memories but also could apply to, e.g., motor skill learning or habit formation.

We considered episodic experiences as collections between categorical variables -- a person, a place, a time of day, and an animal (Fig.~\ref{fig:semantization}A). We implemented learning in the pathways of the PPT as clasifiers trained to predict any missing variable from the others (see Methods Sec.~\ref{ssec:ppt_methods_semantization}), as if one asked ``What animal did you see Bob with in the park at noon?'' (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}B). We then conceptualized an episodic memory as an association of all variables of a single experience (e.g.~Bob, park, noon, Chihuahua), and a semantic generalization as having learned an association that follows a rule and that generalizes to episodes that were not experienced before (e.g., that Bob is always with dogs; Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}B). We then asked how these two types of memory are preserved in either pathway as new episodic memories are continuously stored and consolidated (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}C). Epicsodic memories were acquired during the day by training the indirect pathway classifier to perfectly predict any missing variable. They were then consolidated into a slow learning shortcut classifier at night either by replaying the day's episodes or by random reactivation of the input population (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}D, Methods Sec.~\ref{ssec:ppt_methods_semantization}).

Tracking individual episodic memories over time shows that the indirect pathway acquires their full content during a day but gradually loses the random, contextual detail as new episodes overwrite old ones. The shortcut never acquires the episodic detail in the first place (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}E), because of its low learning rate. When the episodic memories are replayed during consolidation, but not for random reactivations, the shortcut gradually picks up the associations that are consistent across experiences, i.e.~the rules in the environment. While the indirect pathway fails to generalize its learned assocations beyond specific episodic experiences, the shortcut pathway learns semantic generalization that are independent of any specific experience.

However, this result depends on the complexity of the environment. For simpler rules, the indirect pathways can learn semantic generalizations next to the episodic detail, as long as it can remember enough episodes required to extract the rules. And because it can generalize the rule to new episodes, it can provide the correct teaching signal to the shortcut even with random reactivations instead of replay during consolidation.

These results suggest that the same circuit motif that mediates memory transfer also implements a transformation of memory content. Episodic detail is lost in both pathways --- in the indirect pathway through interference, in the shortcut through its slow learning rate --- while the rules of the environment are selectively retained in the shortcut. Replay is not merely beneficial but \emph{necessary} when the regularities of the environment cannot be generalised from a single day's experience by the indirect pathway alone, because random reactivation can only propagate what the indirect pathway has already learned as semantic generalizations.
