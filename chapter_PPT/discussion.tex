%%% DISCUSSION

We proposed the parallel pathway theory (PPT) as a mechanistic basis for systems memory consolidation. This theory relies on two abundant features in the nervous system: parallel shortcut connections between brain areas and Hebbian plasticity.
A mathematical analysis suggests that STDP in a direct pathway achieves consolidation by implementing a linear regression that approximates the input-output mapping of an indirect pathway by that of the direct pathway.
We applied the PPT to hippocampus-dependent memories and showed that the proposed mechanism can transfer memory associations across parallel synaptic pathways. This transfer is robust to different representations in those pathways and requires only weak correlations.
Our results are in quantitative agreement with lesion studies of the perforant path in rodents \cite{Remondes2004} and are able to reproduce forgetting curves that follow a power-law as observed in humans \cite{Wixted2004}.

\subsection{Theory requirements and predictions}
In addition to the anatomical motif of shortcut connections and Hebbian synaptic plasticity, the parallel pathway theory relies on four further requirements during the consolidation phase, which can also be considered as model predictions.

\begin{itemize}
\item[(1)] Temporal correlations between the inputs from the two input pathways are necessary during consolidation, and these correlations should be similar to the ones during storage and recall.
For example,  a consolidation from hippocampus into neocortex would require correlations between cortical and hippocampal activity, as reported in \cite{Ji2007}.
Similarly, a consolidation of spatial memories within the hippocampal formation (including the medial entorhinal cortex, MEC) during replay would require correlations between activity in MEC and hippocampus; in particular, the same locations should be replayed, but represented by grid cells in MEC and by place cells in CA3 and CA1, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}. A significant but weak correlation between the superficial layers of MEC (which provides input to the hippocampus) and CA1 was indeed observed \cite{ONeill2017}. Furthermore, pyramidal cells in the superficial layer III (projecting to CA1, ``direct path'') and stellate cells in the superficial layer II (projecting to DG, which projects to CA3, ``indirect path'') are expected to be correlated due to a strong excitatory feedforward projection from pyramids to stellates \cite{Winterer2017}; reviewed in \cite{Tukker2022}. Coordinated grid and place cell replay was also observed in \cite{Olafsdottir2016} but there CA1 and deep layers of MEC (which receives the hippocampal output) were studied.



\item[(2)] The direct pathway should be plastic during consolidation, while the stored associations in the indirect path remain sufficiently stable
(in contrast to the model in \cite{Tome2020}). In practice, this requires the degree of plasticity to differ between periods of storage and consolidation (e.g., due to neuromodulation \cite{Hasselmo1999a, Papouin2017}), in a potentially pathway-dependent manner. In other words, the requirement is that the content of a memory should not be altered much while creating a backup.

\item[(3)] Plasticity in the shortcut pathway should be driven by a teaching signal from the indirect pathway. This can be achieved by STDP in combination with longer transmission delays in the indirect pathway, as suggested here, but other neural implementations of supervised learning may be equally suitable \cite{Legenstein2005, Pfister2006, Urbanczik2014}.


\item[(4)] Within the present framework, a systematic decrease in learning rates within the consolidation hierarchy (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}) is needed to achieve memory lifetimes on the order of years. That is, synapses involved in later stages of consolidation should be less plastic during consolidation periods such as sleep, as also suggested by \cite{Roxin2013} and \cite{Tome2020}.
Furthermore, Roxin and Fusi elegantly showed in \cite{Roxin2013} that a multistage memory system confers an advantage (in terms of memory lifetime, memory capacity, and initial signal-to-noise ratio) compared to a homogeneous memory system with the same number of synapses, which provides a fundamental computational reason for the existence of a memory consolidation processes at the systems level. However, to be able to exploit this advantage, an efficient mechanism to transfer memories across stages is necessary. The proposed PPT explains how memories can be transferred in a biologically plausible way in a multistage memory system.



Conceptually related to models of systems-memory consolidation with a systematic decrease in learning rates across a hierarchy of networks are models of synaptic memory consolidation with complex synapses that can assume many different states and a decrease of plasticity across a hierarchy of states. In such models of synaptic memory consolidation, also a power-law forgetting has been achieved \cite{Fusi2005, Benna2016}.
Synaptic and systems memory consolidation models are different but not mutually exclusive.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{What limits systems memory consolidation?}
Our account of systems memory consolidation explains how memories are re-organized and transferred across brain regions.
However, certain forms of episodic memory remain hippocampus-dependent throughout life \cite{Winocur2011}.

In the context of the present model, this restriction could result from different factors.
The PPT simplifies memory engrams by replacing multisynaptic by monosynaptic connections whenever possible.
However, a shortcut pathway may not be present anatomically, or it may not host an appropriate representation for a given cue-response association in question.
For example, it may be difficult to consolidate a complex visual object detection task into a shortcut from primary visual cortex (V1) to a decision area because the low-level representation of the visual cue in V1 may not allow it \cite{DiCarlo2007, Majaj2015}.
The same applies to tasks that require a mixed selectivity of neural responses \cite{Rigotti2013}. Such tasks cannot be fully consolidated into shortcuts with simpler representations of cues and/or responses that do not allow a linear separation of the associations. On the basis of similar arguments, early work suggested that the hippocampus could be critical for learning tasks that are not linearly separable \cite{Sutherland1989}.

Within the present framework, the consolidated memory is in essence a linear approximation of the original cue-response association, as indicated in the theoretical analysis in Sec.~\ref{ssec:theory_dynamics}.
The resulting simplification of the memory content could underlie the commonly observed semantization of memories and the loss of episodic detail \cite{Winocur2010, Winocur2011}. Such a semantization could already occur in the earliest shortcut connections \cite{Schapiro2017}, but could also gradually progress in a multi-stage consolidation process. Our simulations confirm this directly (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}): under slow plasticity, the shortcut pathway extracts the linear rule that links person to animal species while the random contextual variables decay --- in the indirect pathway through interference and in the shortcut by never being acquired at all. The mechanism we observe in these simulations is slow plasticity rather than a hard capacity bound; whether and to what extent the shortcut's reduced expressivity also contributes to semantization, independently of plasticity rates, is a quantitative question we leave open.

\subsection{Spatial gradients of semantisation across the consolidation hierarchy}
\label{ssec:semantization_gradient}

The combination of semantisation in a single pathway (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}) and the hierarchical iteration of the PPT (Fig~\ref{fig:hierarchical}) predicts a spatial gradient of memory content along the cascade. Pathways closer to the hippocampus retain more episodic detail, because their higher learning rates allow them to overfit on the day's experience; later pathways, where the learning rates are lower, retain only the statistical regularities of the original associations as semantic content. The hierarchy therefore produces two co-existing gradients in opposite directions: a sharp decay of episodic detail close to the hippocampus and a gradual accumulation of semantic content with distance from it.

This dual gradient offers a mechanistic reading of several otherwise contradictory observations. The trace transformation theory \cite{Winocur2010, Winocur2011} postulates that consolidation leaves episodic memories hippocampus-dependent while extracting their gist into neocortex; the PPT recovers this by setting the early shortcut learning rates high enough to store a limited amount of episodic detail and the later rates low enough that only statistical regularities survive. In the same framework, bilateral hippocampal lesions need not abolish all personal memory, because some episodic content will already have migrated into the first one or two shortcuts (the perforant path being a natural candidate; cf.~\cite{Lux2016}). Likewise, the discrepancy between hippocampus-only retrograde amnesia (covering about five years) and broader medial-temporal-lobe damage (covering decades) reported in \cite{Bayley2006} is consistent with a steeply decaying episodic-content gradient over a short stretch of the cascade. These predictions are speculative but, importantly, anatomically grounded: they predict that \emph{which} shortcut is lesioned matters as much as how much hippocampal tissue is removed.

\subsection{Relation to phenomenological models of systems consolidation}

The basic mechanism of our framework explains memory transfer between brain regions, which is in line with the Standard Consolidation Theory (SCT) \cite{Squire1995, McClelland1995}. Our theoretical framework is closely related to the Complementary Learning Systems Theory (CLST) \cite{McClelland1995, McClelland2013}, which posits that slow and interleaved cortical learning is necessary to avoid catastrophic interference of new memory items with older memories \cite{McCloskey1989}.
In our model, later --- presumably neocortical --- shortcut connections have lower learning rates to achieve longer memory retention times. Interleaved learning could be achieved by interleaved replay \cite{Foster2017, Schuck2019, Liu2019} during consolidation.
Thereby, the results of CLST can be directly applied to learning in shortcuts in our model, such as the rapid neocortical consolidation of new memories that are in line with a previously learned schema \cite{Tse2007, Tse2011, McClelland2013}.

Limitations of memory transfer between brain regions --- as discussed above --- can impair the consolidation process, resulting in memories that remain hippocampus-\allowbreak dependent throughout life.
Hence, our theoretical framework is also in agreement with the Multiple Trace Theory (MTT) \cite{Nadel1997} and the Trace Transformation Theory (TTT) \cite{Winocur2010, Winocur2011}.
The MTT postulates that memories are re-encoded in the hippocampus during retrieval, generating multiple traces for the same memory. Our model maintains multiple memory traces in different shortcut pathways, even without a retrieval-based re-encoding. The consolidation mechanism of the PPT, however, could also transfer a specific memory multiple times if it is re-encoded during retrieval. If neocortex extracts statistical regularities from a collection of memories \cite{McClelland1995}, the consolidation of such a repeatedly re-encoded memory could then lead to a gist-like, more semantic version of that memory in neocortex \cite{Nadel1997, Winocur2011, Levine2002}, as emphasized by the TTT.

The premise of our model is that memories are actively transferred between brain regions. This premise has recently been subject to debate \cite{Yonelinas2019, Antony2019, Yonelinas2019a}, following the suggestion of the Contextual Binding (CB) theory. The CB theory argues that amnesia in lesion studies and replay-like activity can be explained by simultaneous learning in hippocampus and neocortex, together with interference of contextually similar episodic memories \cite{Yonelinas2019}. Note, however, that our framework does not exclude a simultaneous encoding in neocortex and hippocampus, which can be combined with active consolidation \cite{Dudai2015, Pohlchen2020}.

Hence, our mechanistic approach is in agreement with and may allow for a unification of several phenomenological theories of systems consolidation.

\subsection{Relation to recent computational models of selective consolidation}
\label{ssec:relation_selective_consolidation}

Two recent theoretical proposals approach the same question --- why consolidation is selective --- from angles that contrast instructively with the PPT. Sun et al.\ \cite{Sun2023} introduced \emph{generalisation-optimised complementary learning systems} (Go-CLS), in which the amount of cortical consolidation is regulated to maximise the generalisation performance of a linear cortical student trained on hippocampal reactivations. Unpredictable memories do not consolidate, because doing so would harm generalisation; predictable memories consolidate until further reactivation begins to overfit cortical weights to encoding noise. Lindsey et al.\ \cite{Lindsey2024} proposed \emph{recall-gated consolidation}, in which the rate of plasticity in a long-term memory pathway is gated by the recall strength of a short-term pathway: reliable memories accumulate short-term recall over repeated reinforcement and pass the gate, while spurious one-off events do not. Both frameworks render consolidation selective \emph{at the level of individual memories} --- Go-CLS through an upstream optimisation-derived stopping rule, recall-gated consolidation through an explicit downstream plasticity gate driven by a global recall signal.

The PPT shares with these proposals the prediction that consolidation selectively transfers a subset of memory content from hippocampus to cortex, but the mechanism and the granularity differ. In the PPT, every memory acquired in the indirect pathway is exposed to the shortcut by Hebbian plasticity. The shortcut's reduced expressivity and slow plasticity then act as a downstream filter that retains only the linearly approximable, statistically regular component of each association. Selection is therefore architectural rather than gated, and operates \emph{within} each memory rather than \emph{between} memories. The PPT predicts a cortical trace of every memory, consisting of its generalisable component; the gating accounts predict that some memories leave no cortical trace at all. Distinguishing the two regimes experimentally requires assessing whether the cortical contribution to recall is graded by predictability or reliability across a continuum of memories, or instead reflects a sharp gate that excludes some memories entirely.

The proposals are not mutually exclusive. A regulated gate at the hippocampal source --- as in recall-gated consolidation, or in the early-stopping implementations of Go-CLS --- could control \emph{which} reactivations enter the shortcut, while the shortcut's architecture further determines \emph{what survives} of each consolidated reactivation. Indeed, Lindsey et al.\ identify the original PPT \cite{Remme2021} as a ``copying''-style consolidation model that does not implement gating, but the results presented here show that the shortcut mechanism does already perform a form of non-trivial content selection through linearisation and slow plasticity --- a different selection axis from those proposed by Go-CLS and recall-gated consolidation, and one that can operate concurrently with them rather than in competition.

Replay's role also differs across the three frameworks in informative ways. In Go-CLS, replay implements the regulated reactivation count whose length is optimised; in recall-gated consolidation, selective replay of familiar patterns is one of several possible implementations of the gate. In our framework, replay is not regulatory but informational: it is necessary specifically when the indirect-pathway teacher cannot itself generalise the rule from a single day's experience (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}G,H). Random reactivation suffices when the teacher has already extracted the structure; replay becomes essential only when the structure can be learned only by averaging over the specific stimuli that exemplify it. This complements rather than contradicts the gating role of replay in the other proposals.

\subsection{Consolidation of non-declarative memories}

Given that shortcut connections are widespread throughout the central nervous system \cite{VanEssen1992, Morgenstern2016}, the suggested mechanism may also be applicable to the consolidation of non-declarative memories, e.g., of perceptual \cite{Karni1994} and motor skills \cite{Brashers-Krug1996}, fear memory \cite{Kitamura2017} or to the transition of goal-directed to habitual behaviour \cite{Aarts2000}.

Several studies have suggested two-pathway models in the context of motor learning \cite{Makino2016, Pyle2019, Tesileanu2017, Murray2020a}. In particular, Murray and Escola \cite{Murray2020a} recently used a two-pathway model to investigate how repeated practice affects future performance and leads to habitual behaviour.
While their model does not incorporate an active consolidation mechanism or multiple learning stages, the basic mechanism is the same:
A fast learning pathway from cortex to sensorimotor striatum first learns a motor skill and then teaches a slowly learning pathway from thalamus to striatum during subsequent repetition.

\subsection{Limitations of the model and future directions}

The present work focuses on feedforward networks and local learning rules. Hence, the model cannot address how systems memory consolidation affects the representation of sensory stimuli and forms schemata that facilitate future learning \cite{Tse2007, Tse2011} because representation learning typically requires a means of backpropagating information through the system, e.g., by feedback connections \cite{Lillicrap2020}. The interaction of synaptic plasticity with recurrent feedback connections generates a high level of dynamical complexity, which is beyond the scope of the present study. Our framework also does not explain reconsolidation, that is, how previously consolidated memories become labile and hippocampus-dependent again through their reactivation \cite{Debiec2002, Dudai2012}.

On the mechanistic level, the PPT predicts temporally specific deficits in memory consolidation when relevant shortcut connections are lesioned, that is, a tight link between the anatomical organisation of synaptic pathways and their function for memory. These predictions may be most easily tested in non-mammalian systems, where connectomic data are available \cite{Xu2020}.

Our semantisation results (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}) provide a first step in this direction, but they were obtained for a single shortcut motif rather than the full cascade. Simulating the dual gradient of episodic and semantic content that the cascade predicts (Sec.~\ref{ssec:semantization_gradient}) requires extending the consolidation model into the hierarchical setting --- in particular, propagating both the episodic and the semantic component through several shortcut connections with decreasing learning rates. Likewise, the necessity of replay we observe in the single shortcut (Fig~\ref{fig:semantization}H,I) is most striking under one-shot hippocampal acquisition, in which the indirect pathway has had only a single exposure to each memory before the night of consolidation \cite{Lee2015}. If the hippocampus indeed performs one-shot encoding, replay should be required not only at high rule complexity but also for the transfer of \emph{any} regularity into the earliest shortcut. In later stages of the cascade, where the previous shortcut already carries a partial semantic generalisation, random reactivation may again suffice. Bridging the gap between mechanistic synaptic computations and behavioural cognitive function will thus benefit from a cascade-level account of how semantisation, replay, and the architectural reduction of expressivity interact.
